There is a particular kind of urgency in leaving one journey before it has quite ended to begin another. On the final day of the TDMMA workshop at CASA, the closing ceremony was still underway when I had to slip out, gather my equipment, and make for Arambagh. A group from Star Party Bangladesh was waiting, along with an overnight bus and the promise of skies far darker than anything available near Dhaka.

There were about nineteen of us: astronomers, astrophotographers, and people who simply wanted to stand beneath a sky crowded with stars. I was travelling as part of that community, but I also carried a question from my work with CASA: could the remote hills of Bandarban be worth investigating as a future astronomical observing site?

This was not a formal survey. We had no weather station, seeing monitor, or calibrated sky-quality meter. What we had were cameras, field experience, a few nights in the hills, and the chance to pay close attention.

*A night in the hills: the Milky Way above a cottage and the road that carried us deeper into Bandarban.*

## Leaving the city behind

The bus reached Bandarban the following morning after roughly ten hours on the road. From there, travel changed character. We continued toward Ruma in a Chandergari—the four-wheel-drive jeeps used on these mountain routes—and later changed vehicles as the roads became steeper and more demanding.

The landscape opened and folded around us in layers. Roads threaded between green slopes, settlements became smaller, and the visual signs of dense urban infrastructure gradually disappeared. Many paras appeared to consist of only a small number of families. Solar panels were common, while large pools of artificial light were not.

![A Chandergari travelling along a mountain road beneath a gathering storm](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/chandergari-road.webp)

*A Chandergari on the road through the hills, with rain gathering over the distant ridges.*

We stopped at Boga Lake, Munlai Para, Darjeeling Para, and Passing Para as we travelled farther into the hills. At Darjeeling Para we ate locally prepared food; throughout the journey, hospitality and food were as memorable as the landscapes. It is easy to reduce a remote location to a set of observing conditions, but a possible astronomy site is never an empty point on a map. People already live there, work there, and understand the land in ways a visiting team does not.

Any future project would have to begin with those communities—not simply arrive among them.

![Sunlight breaking through clouds over layered mountain ridges](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/sunbeams-over-ridges.webp)

*Sunlight and cloud over the ridges: the same atmosphere that made the days dramatic would repeatedly decide what was possible at night.*

## Sanswang Para: darkness, then cloud

We reached Sanswang Para in the evening and stayed in a wooden cottage powered by solar panels and an inverter. That detail mattered. Remote observing does not necessarily require a conventional electricity grid, provided solar generation, batteries, and power management can support the equipment.

Because we had rented the cottage, nearby lights could be switched off during observations. Once they were, there were very few obvious local light sources. Compared with an urban sky, the darkness felt immediate.

The horizon was less ideal. Mountains enclosed parts of the site, visually blocking perhaps 10–15 degrees in some directions. That is only an estimate, not a measurement; a proper survey would need a horizon profile by azimuth.

And then there was the weather.

The days were intensely hot, yet clouds accumulated toward evening. On the first night, the Milky Way was high during the useful dark window, but clear conditions lasted only about half an hour before cloud ended the main imaging session. Rain followed. Just when we assumed the night was over, the sky opened again and gave us time for star trails.

![Long star trails sweeping above a parked Chandergari](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/chandergari-star-trails.webp)

*After rain interrupted the session, the clearing sky made this long star-trail sequence possible.*

It was a lesson more useful than a perfectly clear night might have been: darkness is only one part of astronomical site quality. Frequent clouds can make an exceptionally dark place scientifically unproductive.

The second night was better, though rain interrupted us again. After it cleared, we returned to imaging. The Galactic centre and the wider band of the Milky Way filled the wide-field frames with structure.

![The Milky Way standing vertically above a mountain ridge and winding road](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/milky-way-ridge.webp)

*The Milky Way above a ridge and winding road. The bright lamps are local foreground sources, not a measurement of the wider sky glow.*

## Daylight between observing sessions

After sleeping late, we woke to cloud moving through the mountains. The hills could change appearance in minutes: hard sunlight on one ridge, mist swallowing another, then rain advancing across the valleys.

![Low cloud and mist surrounding a settlement on a green ridge](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/mist-over-settlement.webp)

*Cloud moving through a hillside settlement on the morning after an observing session.*

Our major daytime trek was to Tlabong waterfall. It took several hours and was physically demanding for some members of the group, but the basin and the spray at the end of the trail offered a complete change of scale after a night spent looking outward into the Galaxy.

![An artistic night composite of the Milky Way above two waterfalls](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/tlabong-night-composite.webp)

*An artistic composite bringing together the waterfall and the night sky; it is a creative landscape image, not a documentary record of the sky from the waterfall at one instant.*

## Keokradong: the sky opens

On the third day we travelled back toward Keokradong, one of Bangladesh’s best-known high peaks. The observing geometry there was markedly different from Sanswang Para. Instead of working from within a bowl of surrounding mountains, we had access—through prior coordination—to a broad, exposed, flat area suitable for tripods and with a much lower apparent horizon.

I am deliberately leaving out operational and access details. Travel in this region involves checkpoints, permissions, and coordination with local communities and authorities. Those constraints would be part of any future scientific programme, just as surely as weather and electricity.

The final night was the best of the expedition. The Milky Way was unmistakable to the naked eye. There were so many visible stars that familiar constellation patterns became harder to pick out. I could also see the Andromeda Galaxy without optical aid, and later photographed it with the Sony camera while it was high in the sky.

Naked-eye Andromeda is consistent with a genuinely dark sky, but it is not a Bortle measurement. Neither are memory, excitement, or a beautiful photograph. Still, standing there under that sky made the central question of the trip feel worth pursuing.

We continued photographing until approximately 4–5 AM and finally slept close to dawn.

## What the photographs tell us—and what they do not

After returning, I analysed seven RAW night-sky frames rather than judging the sites from processed photographs alone. The workflow used black-level-subtracted sensor data, isolated sky regions, and plate solving against a Gaia star catalogue. Every retained frame received a statistically strong plate solution, allowing its celestial pointing to be identified.

The calibrated estimates are preliminary. Among the closest settings-matched frames, the selected Keokradong exposure measured approximately **21.34 Gaia-G-like mag/arcsec²**, while the two Sanswang exposures averaged approximately **21.39**. That difference—about **0.05 magnitude**, or roughly five percent in sky signal—is smaller than the unquantified systematic uncertainty. It does **not** establish that one site is darker.

There are good reasons for restraint:

- the comparable frames point at different regions of the sky;
- all three are on or very close to the Galactic plane, where unresolved Milky Way light affects the background;
- one Sanswang frame points much lower above the horizon than the others;
- clouds, transparency, vignetting, camera response, and atmospheric extinction are not fully controlled;
- the camera clock appears to be offset from local time, making time-derived altitude and twilight calculations provisional.

The Andromeda plate solution provided a useful consistency check. From the Keokradong-area latitude, the photographed field can culminate at about 70 degrees altitude—visually close to overhead, though still about 20 degrees from the geometric zenith. That agrees with the field recollection better than the uncorrected EXIF timestamp does.

So the honest conclusion is not a Bortle number and not a winner between two sites. It is this: **the photographs support the impression of very dark conditions, while also showing why a controlled survey is necessary.**

## The morning after

Morning at Keokradong was almost as memorable as the night. Mist moved around the peaks, cultivated slopes caught sudden patches of sunlight, and distant ridges dissolved into blue-grey layers. The same atmosphere that made the landscape beautiful also kept reminding us that observing reliability would need to be measured over months, not guessed from a few nights.

The landscape made the attraction of a mountain observatory easy to understand. Elevation can reduce the atmospheric column above an observer and may help with extinction, haze, and transparency. But a mountain does not automatically provide good astronomical seeing. Local terrain, wind, boundary-layer turbulence, humidity, and high-altitude air all matter.

That distinction—between a promising place and a proven site—is the heart of this story.

## What should happen next

A proper campaign should observe both locations repeatedly across seasons. It should measure sky brightness with a calibrated Sky Quality Meter, record cloud fraction and weather continuously, map the horizon, estimate atmospheric extinction, and use a seeing monitor such as a DIMM. Camera comparisons should use the same high-altitude, high-Galactic-latitude field from both sites, with identical settings and multiple repetitions.

Infrastructure needs equal attention: road access, stable power, communications, equipment transport, permissions, environmental impact, and long-term maintenance. Any future facility would require meaningful consultation with local residents and relevant authorities, and it should create local value through education, employment, collaboration, responsible tourism, or dark-sky preservation.

We returned home with memory cards full of mountains and stars, but not with a verdict. That is a good outcome. The remote hill tracts showed enough darkness, elevation, and open sky to justify better questions and better instruments.

Could this region support serious astronomical observing in Bangladesh? We do not know yet.

But now we have a reason to find out.
