There is a particular kind of urgency in leaving one journey before it has quite ended to begin another. On the final day of the TDMMA workshop at CASA, the closing ceremony was still underway when I had to slip out, gather my equipment, and make for Arambagh. A group from Star Party Bangladesh was waiting, along with an overnight bus and the promise of skies far darker than anything available near Dhaka.

There were about nineteen of us: astrophotographers, and people who simply wanted to stand beneath a sky crowded with stars. I was travelling as part of that community, but I also carried a question from my work with CASA: could the remote hills of Bandarban be worth investigating as a future astronomical observing site?

This was not a formal survey. We had no weather station, seeing monitor, or calibrated sky-quality meter. What we had were cameras, field experience, a few nights in the hills, and the chance to pay close attention.

*A night in the hills: the Milky Way above a cottage and the road that carried us deeper into Bandarban.*

## Leaving the city behind

The bus reached Bandarban the following morning after roughly ten hours on the road. From there, travel changed character. We continued toward Ruma in a Chandergari—the four-wheel-drive jeeps used on these mountain routes—and later changed vehicles as the roads became steeper and more demanding.

The landscape opened and folded around us in layers. Roads threaded between green slopes, settlements became smaller, and the visual signs of dense urban infrastructure gradually disappeared. Many paras appeared to consist of only a small number of families. Solar panels were common, while large pools of artificial light were not. Across the places we visited, I did not see high-rise buildings at all. That absence matters astronomically: there were no towers filling the horizon with windows, façade lighting, billboards, or the concentrated glow familiar in a city.

![A Chandergari travelling along a mountain road beneath a gathering storm](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/chandergari-road.webp)

*A Chandergari on the road through the hills, with rain gathering over the distant ridges.*

We stopped at Boga Lake, Munlai Para, Darjeeling Para, and Passing Para as we travelled farther into the hills. At Darjeeling Para we ate locally prepared food; throughout the journey, hospitality and food were as memorable as the landscapes. It is easy to reduce a remote location to a set of observing conditions, but a possible astronomy site is never an empty point on a map. People already live there, work there, and understand the land in ways a visiting team does not.

Any future project would have to begin with those communities—not simply arrive among them.

![Sunlight breaking through clouds over layered mountain ridges](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/sunbeams-over-ridges.webp)

*Sunlight and cloud over the ridges: the same atmosphere that made the days dramatic would repeatedly decide what was possible at night.*

## Sanswang Para: darkness, then cloud

We reached Sanswang Para in the evening and stayed in a wooden cottage powered by solar panels and an inverter. That detail mattered. Remote observing does not necessarily require a conventional electricity grid, provided solar generation, batteries, and power management can support the equipment.

Because we had rented the cottage, nearby lights could be switched off during observations. Once they were, there were very few obvious local light sources. Compared with Dhaka and the urban or semi-urban places where I usually observe, the difference was miles apart visually. The darkness was not subtle: our eyes adapted more deeply, the Milky Way carried more contrast, and the sky no longer looked like a grey background behind the brightest stars. This is a firsthand visual description, not a calibrated claim that light pollution was literally zero, but obvious local light pollution was remarkably absent.

The horizon was less ideal. Mountains enclosed parts of the site, visually blocking perhaps 10–15 degrees in some directions. That is only an estimate, not a measurement; a proper survey would need a horizon profile by azimuth.

And then there was the weather.

The days were intensely hot, yet clouds accumulated toward evening. On the first night, the Milky Way was high during the useful dark window, but clear conditions lasted only about half an hour before cloud ended the main imaging session. Rain followed. Just when we assumed the night was over, the sky opened again and gave us time for star trails.

![Long star trails sweeping above a parked Chandergari](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/chandergari-star-trails.webp)

*After rain interrupted the session, the clearing sky made this long star-trail sequence possible.*

It was a lesson more useful than a perfectly clear night might have been: darkness is only one part of astronomical site quality. Frequent clouds can make an exceptionally dark place scientifically unproductive.

The second night was better, though rain interrupted us again. After it cleared, we returned to imaging. The Galactic centre and the wider band of the Milky Way filled the wide-field frames with structure.


## Daylight between observing sessions

After sleeping late, we woke to cloud moving through the mountains. The hills could change appearance in minutes: hard sunlight on one ridge, mist swallowing another, then rain advancing across the valleys.

![Low cloud and mist surrounding a settlement on a green ridge](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/mist-over-settlement.webp)

*Cloud moving through a hillside settlement on the morning after an observing session.*

Our major daytime trek was to Tlabong waterfall. It took several hours and was physically demanding for some members of the group, but the basin and the spray at the end of the trail offered a complete change of scale after a night spent looking outward into the Galaxy.

![An artistic night composite of the Milky Way above two waterfalls](/blog/bandarban-dark-skies/tlabong-night-composite.webp)

## Keokradong: the sky opens

On the third day we travelled back toward Keokradong, one of Bangladesh’s best-known high peaks. The observing geometry there was markedly different from Sanswang Para. Instead of working from within a bowl of surrounding mountains, we had access—through prior coordination—to a broad, exposed, flat area suitable for tripods and with a much lower apparent horizon. The elevation gave the location a practical edge: more of the sky was available above the surrounding terrain, low-level haze appeared less dominant, and targets could be followed across a wider arc. Elevation is a reason to test the site, although it does not by itself prove good atmospheric seeing.

I am deliberately leaving out operational and access details. Travel in this region involves checkpoints, permissions, and coordination with local communities, the Bangladesh Army, Border Guard Bangladesh, and other relevant authorities. Defence control and an established security presence could offer an important layer of protection for visitors and expensive instruments. At the same time, it creates an operational requirement: access, construction, long-term monitoring, and every organised observing programme would need formal permission and continuing coordination. Security presence is an advantage to investigate, not an automatic guarantee.

The final night was the best of the expedition. The Milky Way was not merely a pale band: its bright core and structure were visible with the naked eye. There were so many visible stars that familiar constellation patterns became harder to pick out. I could also see the Andromeda Galaxy without optical aid, then point it out to others before photographing it high in the sky. Those were deeply human moments—the group growing quiet, eyes adjusting, someone recognising a faint object that had travelled 2.5 million years to reach us. They conveyed the darkness more directly than a number could in the field.

The naked-eye Milky Way core, naked-eye Andromeda, and near absence of obvious artificial glow are all strong qualitative indicators of a dark place. They are not, individually or together, an exact Bortle measurement. Neither are memory, excitement, or a beautiful photograph. Still, standing there under that sky made the central question of the trip feel worth pursuing.

We continued photographing until approximately 4–5 AM and finally slept close to dawn.

## The camera kit and how I exposed the sky

I travelled with a compact Sony APS-C system:

- **Sony α6400** camera;
- **Sigma 16 mm f/1.4** for the wide Milky Way and landscape frames;
- **Sigma 56 mm f/1.4** for a tighter view of the Andromeda field;
- a **K&F Concept tripod** for the single exposures and longer sequences.

The wide-field comparison frames were captured in RAW with the 16 mm lens wide open at **f/1.4**. The closest settings-matched group used **10-second exposures at ISO 3200**. Ten seconds collected substantial sky signal while keeping stellar motion reasonably controlled at 16 mm. I checked focus on bright stars at high magnification, reviewed the histogram and bright foreground lamps for clipping, and adjusted ISO rather than changing several variables at once. For the tighter Andromeda frame, the 56 mm lens required a shorter **4-second exposure at ISO 6400** to limit trailing while keeping the galaxy and surrounding stars recorded.

That field procedure is about balancing four things: aperture gathers light; exposure time builds signal but also lengthens star trails; ISO changes the recorded amplification and highlight headroom; and focal length determines how quickly Earth’s rotation becomes visible. Recording RAW files preserved the linear sensor information needed for later comparison.

## What the photographs tell us—and what they do not

After returning, I analysed seven RAW night-sky frames rather than judging the sites from processed photographs alone. First, I inventoried the exposure time, aperture, ISO, focal length, lens, and capture time for every frame. I compared photographs directly only when those settings matched. The camera’s recorded black level was subtracted from the Bayer sensor values so that electronic offset was not mistaken for sky brightness.

Foreground mountains, roads, buildings, and lamps were then excluded with sky-only masks. Within each accepted sky area, I divided the image into tiles and used the darker, robustly selected tiles to reduce the effect of stars, the Milky Way, clouds, and local gradients. The resulting background rate was normalised by exposure time.

Next, each photograph was plate-solved against a Gaia star catalogue. The solution identified the celestial coordinates and angular area represented by each pixel. Aperture measurements of matched stars provided a provisional Gaia-G-like photometric zero point, allowing the sensor background to be expressed as an estimated sky surface brightness in magnitudes per square arcsecond. This is much more informative than comparing the brightness of edited JPEGs, but dark frames, flat fields, camera-response characterisation, extinction measurements, and repeated matched pointings are still needed to control the systematic error.

The calibrated estimates are preliminary. Among the closest settings-matched frames, the selected Keokradong exposure measured approximately **21.34 Gaia-G-like mag/arcsec²**, while the two Sanswang exposures averaged approximately **21.39**. That difference—about **0.05 magnitude**, or roughly five percent in sky signal—is smaller than the unquantified systematic uncertainty. It does **not** establish that one site is darker.

So the honest conclusion is not a Bortle number and not a winner between two sites. It is this: **the photographs support the impression of very dark conditions, while also showing why a controlled survey is necessary.**

## The morning after

Morning at Keokradong was almost as memorable as the night. Mist moved around the peaks, cultivated slopes caught sudden patches of sunlight, and distant ridges dissolved into blue-grey layers. The same atmosphere that made the landscape beautiful also kept reminding us that observing reliability would need to be measured over months, not guessed from a few nights.

The landscape made the attraction of a mountain observatory easy to understand. Elevation can reduce the atmospheric column above an observer and may help with extinction, haze, and transparency. But a mountain does not automatically provide good astronomical seeing. Local terrain, wind, boundary-layer turbulence, humidity, and high-altitude air all matter.

That distinction—between a promising place and a proven site—is the heart of this story.

## What should happen next

A proper campaign should observe both locations repeatedly across seasons. It should measure sky brightness with a calibrated Sky Quality Meter, record cloud fraction and weather continuously, map the horizon, estimate atmospheric extinction, and use a seeing monitor such as a DIMM. Camera comparisons should use the same high-altitude, high-Galactic-latitude field from both sites, with identical settings and multiple repetitions.

Infrastructure needs equal attention: road access, stable power, communications, equipment transport, permissions, environmental impact, and long-term maintenance. Any future facility would require meaningful consultation with local residents and relevant authorities, and it should create local value through education, employment, collaboration, responsible tourism, or dark-sky preservation.

There is also a possibility broader than a single observatory: a **dark-sky reserve in Bangladesh**. The combination we experienced—very little obvious artificial glow, no high-rise development in the visited area, dramatic mountain horizons, established routes, and communities already hosting visitors—makes the region worth evaluating for protected-dark-sky status. Such a reserve could support astronomy education, responsible astrotourism, local guiding and employment, and the preservation of a night environment that is becoming rare elsewhere.

That idea would require far more than declaring the sky dark. It would need measured boundaries, lighting policies developed with residents, long-term sky monitoring, environmental assessment, community consent and benefit, and coordination with civil and defence authorities. The goal should be to protect both the sky and the interests of the people who live beneath it.

We returned home with memory cards full of mountains and stars, but not with a verdict. That is a good outcome. The remote hill tracts showed enough darkness, elevation, and open sky to justify better questions and better instruments.

Could this region support serious astronomical observing in Bangladesh? We do not know yet.

But now we have a reason to find out.
